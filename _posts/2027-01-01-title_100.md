To verify that the unequal flow split explanation is consistent with the experimental tracer data, we can break the analysis down into two steps: finding the actual flow split from the RTD moments, and calculating the resulting conversion.
------------------------------
## Step 1: Find the Flow Split from RTD Moments
Let the total volume be $V_{\text{total}} = 2V$ and the total flow rate be $v$. For parallel systems, the mean residence time ($\bar{t}$) is:
$$\bar{t} = \frac{MO_1}{MO_0} = \frac{120}{1} = 120\text{ s}$$ 
Since $\bar{t} = \frac{V_{\text{total}}}{v} = \frac{2V}{v} = 120\text{ s}$, the space time for an equal ideal split is $\tau_{\text{avg}} = \frac{V}{v} = 60\text{ s}$.
If the flow is maldistributed, let a fraction $\alpha$ go to Reactor 1 and $(1-\alpha)$ go to Reactor 2. The individual reactor residence times become:
$$\tau_1 = \frac{60}{\alpha} \quad \text{and} \quad \tau_2 = \frac{60}{1-\alpha}$$ 
The second raw moment ($M_2$) for two parallel CSTRs is given by the flow-weighted sum of their individual second moments ($2\tau_i^2$):
$$M_2 = \frac{MO_2}{MO_0} = \alpha(2\tau_1^2) + (1-\alpha)(2\tau_2^2)$$ 
$$4.501 \times 10^4 = 2 \left[ \alpha \left(\frac{60}{\alpha}\right)^2 + (1-\alpha)\left(\frac{60}{1-\alpha}\right)^2 \right] = 7200 \left[ \frac{1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)} \right]$$ 
Solving for $\alpha(1-\alpha)$:
$$\alpha(1-\alpha) = \frac{7200}{45010} \approx 0.160$$ 
$$\alpha^2 - \alpha + 0.160 = 0 \implies \alpha = 0.2 \text{ or } 0.8$$ 
The tracer study confirms that the flow is severely maldistributed with an 80/20 split.
------------------------------
## Step 2: Verify the Resulting Conversion
First, determine the reaction rate constant ($k$) using the ideal design specifications (equal 50/50 split where $\tau = 120\text{ s}$ and conversion $X = 0.90$):
$$X_{\text{design}} = \frac{k\tau}{1 + k\tau} \implies 0.90 = \frac{120k}{1 + 120k}$$ 
$$0.90 + 108k = 120k \implies k = 0.075\text{ s}^{-1}$$ 
Now, compute the actual space times and conversions for each reactor under the 80/20 flow split ($\alpha = 0.2$):

* Reactor 1 ($\alpha = 0.2$):
$$\tau_1 = \frac{60}{0.2} = 300\text{ s} \implies X_1 = \frac{0.075(300)}{1 + 0.075(300)} = \frac{22.5}{23.5} \approx 0.9575$$ 
* Reactor 2 ($1-\alpha = 0.8$):
$$\tau_2 = \frac{60}{0.8} = 75\text{ s} \implies X_2 = \frac{0.075(75)}{1 + 0.075(75)} = \frac{5.625}{6.625} \approx 0.8491$$ 
* Overall Conversion ($X_{\text{total}}$):
$$X_{\text{total}} = \alpha X_1 + (1-\alpha) X_2$$ 
$$X_{\text{total}} = 0.2(0.9575) + 0.8(0.8491) = 0.1915 + 0.6793 = \mathbf{0.871}$$ 

## Conclusion
The calculated overall conversion of 0.87 exactly matches the observed under-performance. Therefore, the explanation that a faulty flow splitter causes an uneven 80/20 flow distribution is fully consistent with the experimental measurements.

## 1. Set Up and Group Exponents
Start with the standard convolution integral:
$$E(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_1 \tau_2} \int_{0}^{t} e^{-\lambda/\tau_1} e^{-(t - \lambda)/\tau_2} d\lambda$$ 
Pull out the constant $e^{-t/\tau_2}$ and group the $\lambda$ terms into a single base exponent:
$$E(t) = \frac{e^{-t/\tau_2}}{\tau_1 \tau_2} \int_{0}^{t} e^{\lambda \left(\frac{1}{\tau_2} - \frac{1}{\tau_1}\right)} d\lambda$$ 
------------------------------
## 2. Define a Temporary Constant
To avoid carrying messy fractions through the integration, let $a = \frac{1}{\tau_2} - \frac{1}{\tau_1} = \frac{\tau_1 - \tau_2}{\tau_1 \tau_2}$.
Now integrate $e^{a\lambda}$ directly:
$$E(t) = \frac{e^{-t/\tau_2}}{\tau_1 \tau_2} \left[ \frac{e^{a\lambda}}{a} \right]_{0}^{t} = \frac{e^{-t/\tau_2}}{\tau_1 \tau_2 \cdot a} \left( e^{at} - 1 \right)$$ 
------------------------------
## 3. Substitute and Simplify
Notice that the front denominator simplifies perfectly: $\tau_1 \tau_2 \cdot a = \tau_1 \tau_2 \left(\frac{\tau_1 - \tau_2}{\tau_1 \tau_2}\right) = \tau_1 - \tau_2$.
Substitute this back in and distribute $e^{-t/\tau_2}$:
$$E(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_1 - \tau_2} \left( e^{-t/\tau_2} \cdot e^{\left(\frac{1}{\tau_2} - \frac{1}{\tau_1}\right)t} - e^{-t/\tau_2} \right)$$ 
$$E(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_1 - \tau_2} \left( e^{-t/\tau_2 + t/\tau_2 - t/\tau_1} - e^{-t/\tau_2} \right)$$ 
------------------------------
## ✅ Final Time-Domain Result
$$E(t) = \frac{1}{\tau_1 - \tau_2} \left( e^{-t/\tau_1} - e^{-t/\tau_2} \right)$$

The dead volume does not appear explicitly as an isolated variable in the final $F(t)$ equation. Instead, it is implicitly embedded inside the exponential time constant ($\tau_{\text{act}}$) of the well-mixed CSTR term.
Here is exactly how it affects the math:
## 1. The Dynamic Link to $\tau_{\text{act}}$
The exponential portion of the $F$-curve equation is written as:
$$F(t) = 0.3 + 0.7\left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{t-5}{\tau_{\text{act}}}\right)\right]$$ 
From your problem, the experimental time constant is $\tau_{\text{act}} = 8\text{ min}$. This active time constant is directly defined by the active mixing volume ($V_{\text{act}}$) and the active branch flow rate ($v_1$):
$$\tau_{\text{act}} = \frac{V_{\text{act}}}{v_1} = \frac{V_{\text{total}} - V_{\text{pfr}} - \mathbf{V_{\text{dead}}}}{v_1}$$ 
## 2. Expressing $F(t)$ with Dead Volume Shown Explicitly
If you substitute the volume variables back into the denominator of the exponent, the structural equation reveals exactly where $V_{\text{dead}}$ hides:
$$F(t) = 0.3 + 0.7\left[1 - \exp\left(-\frac{(t-5) \cdot v_1}{V_{\text{total}} - V_{\text{pfr}} - \mathbf{V_{\text{dead}}}}\right)\right]$$ 
## 3. The Physical Effect on the Graph
Because a portion of the reactor volume is stagnant ($\mathbf{V_{\text{dead}} = 0.59\text{ L}}$), the active volume ($V_{\text{act}}$) is smaller than it ideally should be.

* Smaller Denominator: A smaller active volume makes the denominator inside the exponent smaller.
* Faster Rise: This forces the exponential term to decay much faster, which causes the $F(t)$ curve to rise much more steeply toward 1.0 than a fully active, ideal reactor would.

For a second-order reaction with the rate law $-r_A = k C_A^2$, the calculation changes because conversion becomes dependent on the entering reactant concentration ($C_{A0}$). We track the concentrations through both parallel paths step-by-step.
------------------------------
## 1. Deriving the Equations for the Non-Ideal Reactor

* Path 1 (Bypass Branch):
* Carries $30\%$ of the flow.
   * No reaction occurs: $C_{A1} = C_{A0}$.
* Path 2 (Main Reacting Branch):
* Carries $70\%$ of the flow.
   * Step 1: PFR Section ($\tau_p = 5\text{ min}$)
   Using the performance equation for a second-order reaction in a PFR:
   $$\frac{1}{C_{A,\text{pfr}}} - \frac{1}{C_{A0}} = k \tau_p \implies C_{A,\text{pfr}} = \frac{C_{A0}}{1 + k C_{A0} \tau_p}$$ 
   * Step 2: Active CSTR Section ($\tau_m = 8\text{ min}$)
   Using the performance equation for a second-order reaction in a CSTR:
   $$\tau_m = \frac{C_{A,\text{pfr}} - C_{A2}}{k C_{A2}^2} \implies k \tau_m C_{A2}^2 + C_{A2} - C_{A,\text{pfr}} = 0$$ 
   Solving the quadratic equation for the positive real concentration $C_{A2}$:
   $$C_{A2} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 k \tau_m C_{A,\text{pfr}}}}{2 k \tau_m}$$ 
* Final Mixing at the Outlet:
$$C_{A,\text{out}} = 0.3 C_{A0} + 0.7 C_{A2}$$ 
The overall conversion is:
$$X_{\text{non-ideal}} = 1 - \frac{C_{A,\text{out}}}{C_{A0}} = 0.7 \left( 1 - \frac{C_{A2}}{C_{A0}} \right)$$ 

------------------------------
## 2. Comparison with an Ideal CSTR
An ideal CSTR with a nominal residence time of $\tau_{\text{ideal}} = 15\text{ min}$ satisfies:
$$k \tau_{\text{ideal}} C_{A,\text{ideal}}^2 + C_{A,\text{ideal}} - C_{A0} = 0$$ 
Solving for the ideal exit concentration:
$$C_{A,\text{ideal}} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 k \tau_{\text{ideal}} C_{A0}}}{2 k \tau_{\text{ideal}}}$$ 
------------------------------
## 3. Numerical Example (Using $k C_{A0} = 0.1\text{ min}^{-1}$)
To illustrate the performance drop, let's assume a standard feed capacity where $C_{A0} = 1.0\text{ mol/L}$ and $k = 0.1\text{ L/(mol}\cdot\text{min)}$.
## Ideal CSTR Performance:
$$C_{A,\text{ideal}} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(0.1)(15)}}{2(0.1)(15)} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{7}}{3} \approx 0.549\text{ mol/L}$$ 
$$X_{\text{ideal}} = 1 - \frac{0.549}{1.0} = \mathbf{0.451 \quad (45.1\% \text{ Conversion})}$$ 
## Non-Ideal Reactor Performance:

   1. Exit of PFR:
   $$C_{A,\text{pfr}} = \frac{1.0}{1 + (0.1)(5)} = \frac{1.0}{1.5} \approx 0.667\text{ mol/L}$$ 
   2. Exit of active CSTR:
   $$C_{A2} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(0.1)(8)(0.667)}}{2(0.1)(8)} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{3.134}}{1.6} \approx 0.481\text{ mol/L}$$ 
   3. Total mixed outlet concentration:
   $$C_{A,\text{out}} = 0.3(1.0) + 0.7(0.481) = 0.3 + 0.337 = 0.637\text{ mol/L}$$ 
   4. Total conversion:
   $$X_{\text{non-ideal}} = 1 - \frac{0.637}{1.0} = \mathbf{0.363 \quad (36.3\% \text{ Conversion})}$$ 

## Key Takeaway for Higher-Order Reactions
For a second-order reaction, the performance penalty is even more severe. While the first-order reaction conversion dropped by roughly $13.6\%$ in absolute terms ($60\% \to 46.4\%$), the second-order reaction drops by a larger relative amount due to the concentration dependency of the rate law. Because the bypass dilutes the active stream heavily at the output, and higher-order reactions run significantly slower at lower reactant concentrations, the bypass and dead volume non-idealities severely limit chemical yields.
